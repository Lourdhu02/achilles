\begin{qa}[Classical Corner Detection \& Invariant Descriptors]
\Q{How do you recover depth from a rectified stereo pair, and why does depth error grow as $Z^2$?}{
After rectification the epipolar lines are horizontal, so a point at depth $Z$ appears at $x_L$ and $x_R$ on the same row with disparity $d = x_L - x_R$. Similar triangles give
$$Z = \frac{f\,B}{d} \qquad (f \text{ in pixels}, B \text{ baseline in metres})$$
\textbf{Error propagation}: $\left|\frac{\partial Z}{\partial d}\right| = \frac{fB}{d^2} = \frac{Z^2}{fB} \implies \Delta Z \approx \frac{Z^2}{fB}\,\Delta d$.\\
\textbf{Example}: $f = 1000$\,px, $B = 0.12$\,m, matching error $\Delta d = 0.25$\,px. At $Z = 10$\,m, $\Delta Z \approx 0.21$\,m; at $Z = 40$\,m, $\Delta Z \approx 3.3$\,m (16$\times$ worse for 4$\times$ the range).\\
\textbf{Levers}: wider baseline (more occlusion, harder matching), longer focal length (narrower field of view), sub-pixel matching (SGM, RAFT-Stereo). Without rectification, \textbf{triangulate} by DLT: each view gives $x \times (P X) = 0$, two linear equations; stack and solve $A X = 0$ by SVD, then refine by minimizing reprojection error.}

\Q{How does SIFT achieve scale invariance, and why does Lowe's Ratio Test reject false matches?}{
\textbf{Scale Invariance}: SIFT builds an octave scale space by convolving the image with Gaussians of increasing variance $\sigma$, then computes Difference-of-Gaussians (DoG):
$$D(x, y, \sigma) = (G(x, y, k\sigma) - G(x, y, \sigma)) * I(x, y)$$
Extrema (minima/maxima) are detected by comparing each pixel against its 8 neighbors in the current scale and $9+9=18$ neighbors in adjacent scales (26 comparisons). Sub-pixel localization fits a 3D quadratic Taylor polynomial.\\[2pt]
\textbf{Lowe's Ratio Test}: When matching keypoint descriptor $d_A$ against target set, let $d_{B1}$ be the nearest neighbor ($L_2$ distance $r_1$) and $d_{B2}$ be the second-nearest neighbor ($L_2$ distance $r_2$).
$$\text{Ratio} = \frac{r_1}{r_2} < \tau \quad (\tau \approx 0.75-0.80)$$
Ambiguous/repetitive features (e.g., foliage, building windows) produce $r_1 \approx r_2 \implies \text{Ratio} \approx 1.0$ and are discarded; unique salient features yield $r_1 \ll r_2$, ensuring high-confidence matching.}

\Q{Derive the RANSAC iteration formula for geometric model fitting (e.g., Homography or Epipolar).}{
Given sample size $s$ ($s=4$ for Homography, $s=8$ for Fundamental Matrix), outlier ratio $\epsilon \in (0, 1)$, and desired probability of success $p$ (typically $0.99$):
The probability that a random draw of $s$ points contains only inliers is $(1 - \epsilon)^s$.
The probability that at least one of $N$ random trials draws an all-inlier set is:
$$1 - (1 - (1 - \epsilon)^s)^N = p \implies N = \frac{\ln(1 - p)}{\ln(1 - (1 - \epsilon)^s)}$$
\textbf{Example}: For $\epsilon = 0.5$ (50\% outliers) and Homography ($s=4$):
$(1 - \epsilon)^4 = (0.5)^4 = 0.0625$. For $p = 0.99$: $N = \frac{\ln(0.01)}{\ln(1 - 0.0625)} \approx \frac{-4.605}{-0.0645} \approx 72\text{ iterations}$.}
\end{qa}

\begin{qa}[Epipolar Geometry \& Optical Flow]
\Q{How does Zhang's camera calibration work, and what does PnP solve?}{
\textbf{Zhang (2000)}: photograph a planar checkerboard ($Z = 0$) in $\ge 3$ poses. Each view gives a homography $H = \lambda K[r_1\;r_2\;t]$. Because $r_1 \perp r_2$ and $\|r_1\| = \|r_2\|$, each view adds two linear constraints on the symmetric $B = K^{-\top}K^{-1}$:
$$h_1^\top B\,h_2 = 0, \qquad h_1^\top B\,h_1 = h_2^\top B\,h_2$$
$B$ has 6 unknowns up to scale, so 3 views suffice. Recover $K$ from $B$ (Cholesky), then refine $K$, lens distortion $(k_1, k_2, p_1, p_2)$ and all poses with Levenberg--Marquardt on reprojection error (good calibration: $< 0.3$\,px RMS).\\[2pt]
\textbf{PnP (Perspective-n-Point)}: given known $K$ and $n$ 3D--2D matches, recover the 6-DoF pose $[R \mid t]$. Minimal P3P uses 3 points (up to 4 solutions; a 4th point disambiguates); EPnP is $\mathcal{O}(n)$. Wrap in RANSAC, then refine by minimizing reprojection error. Used for AR anchoring, visual relocalization and robot grasping.}

\Q{How does RAFT estimate optical flow, and why does it beat coarse-to-fine pyramids?}{
Classical flow (Q4) linearizes brightness constancy, so it only works for small motions and needs image pyramids, which blur away small fast objects.
\textbf{RAFT (Teed \& Deng, 2020)}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Extracts $1/8$-resolution features $f(I_1), f(I_2)$ and builds a 4D \textbf{all-pairs correlation volume} $C_{ijkl} = \langle f_1(i,j), f_2(k,l)\rangle$, pooled into a 4-level pyramid over the last two dims.
\item A recurrent \textbf{ConvGRU} iteratively updates a single high-resolution flow field, $\mathbf{f}_{k+1} = \mathbf{f}_k + \Delta \mathbf{f}$, by looking up correlations around the current estimate (radius 4, all pyramid levels).
\item Loss: $\sum_k \gamma^{N-k}\|\mathbf{f}_{\text{gt}} - \mathbf{f}_k\|_1$ with $\gamma = 0.8$ over 12 training iterations.
\end{enumerate}
Large displacements are visible from step 0 through the coarse correlation levels, yet flow is never estimated at low resolution, so thin and fast objects survive. Cost: $\mathcal{O}((HW)^2)$ memory for the volume.}
\end{qa}

\begin{trap}[Classical CV Production Traps]
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Raw 8-Point Algorithm Failure}: Unnormalized coordinates cause numerical instability ($x \sim 10^3 \implies x_1 x_2 \sim 10^6$ while constant is $1$). Always apply Hartley's normalization: translate centroid to origin and scale average distance to $\sqrt{2}$.
\item \textbf{Hamming vs Euclidean Distance}: ORB descriptors are binary strings; using Euclidean distance ($L_2$) produces nonsense. Always use Hamming distance via hardware \texttt{POPCNT(a \^{} b)}.
\end{itemize}
\end{trap}
