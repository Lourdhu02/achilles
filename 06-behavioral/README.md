## Why behavioral interviews matter MORE than people think

Common myth: "Just be yourself."

Reality: behavioral interviews are 30-50% of your interview score.

The rubric:
- **Did the candidate provide specific, structured examples?** (Vague stories = automatic fail)
- **Do the examples show the qualities we want (leadership, impact, growth)?**
- **Are they self-aware about failures + learning?**
- **Would they fit our team's culture?**

You can ace every coding round and still lose the offer because your behavioral answers were rambling, vague, or culture-misaligned.

## The two types of behavioral questions

### Type 1: STAR questions ("Tell me about a time...")
- "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager."
- "Tell me about a project that failed."
- "Tell me about a time you handled ambiguity."

**Approach:** STAR framework (`01-STAR-framework.md`). Have a curated story bank of 15-25 stories ready.

### Type 2: Opinion/values questions ("How do you think about X?")
- "How do you stay current with ML?"
- "What's the most exciting thing happening in AI right now?"
- "If you had unlimited resources, what would you build?"

**Approach:** Have prepared, thoughtful, personal answers. Not generic.

## Your story bank: the most important behavioral asset

You need ~20 high-quality STAR stories. For each interview prep session, pull relevant ones.

**Build the bank in `04-your-story-bank.md` over Months 6-10.**

Recommended story themes:
1. A complex technical project (your proudest)
2. A project that failed
3. A disagreement with a manager / coworker
4. A time you took ownership of something outside your role
5. A time you handled ambiguity
6. A time you had to learn something completely new fast
7. A time you missed a deadline
8. A time you delivered impact under constraint
9. A time you mentored someone
10. A time you influenced without authority
11. A time you said no / pushed back on scope
12. A time you simplified something complex
13. A time you fixed something nobody asked you to
14. A time customer feedback changed your approach
15. A time you turned around a struggling project
16. A time you made a controversial call
17. A time you decided to STOP a project
18. A time you scaled something 10x
19. A time you improved team velocity
20. A time you handled criticism / negative feedback

For each, write down 1-2 candidate stories from your life. Then pick the best, develop in STAR format.

## The compounding effect

Behavioral stories COMPOUND across interviews. Each story:
- Can be reused in 5-10 different questions
- Gets sharper with each retelling
- Builds confidence (you've already "tested" it)

**Investment of 20-30 hours in story development = ROI across every interview for the next decade.**

## Mistakes that kill behavioral rounds

### Mistake 1: Vagueness
Bad: "I worked on a project and it went well."
Good: "In Q3 2025, I led the OCR pipeline rewrite at Sujanix. The challenge was handling motion blur in real-world meter images. I proposed using FocalCTCLoss to handle class imbalance in the character distribution..."

Specific details build credibility. Anonymous projects don't.

### Mistake 2: All "I" or all "we"
Bad: "We built a system that did X" (where's your contribution?)
Bad: "I did everything single-handedly" (no team mentioned, suspicious)
Good: "I led the architecture; our two engineers implemented the data pipeline; I personally implemented the FocalCTCLoss component"

Make YOUR contribution legible without erasing teammates.

### Mistake 3: No outcome
Bad: "I worked hard on it."
Good: "The new pipeline achieved 97% exact-match accuracy, up from our baseline of 89%. This unlocked the government utility contract worth ₹X."

NUMBERS, RESULTS, IMPACT. Always.

### Mistake 4: No reflection
Bad: "And it worked great."
Good: "Looking back, I'd have done the data audit earlier — we spent 2 weeks on a model architecture that didn't help because the issue was a labeling inconsistency. Lesson: always validate data before tuning models."

Show you LEARN from experiences.

### Mistake 5: Failing to connect to the role
Bad: Random war story.
Good: "I bring this up because [Company]'s team is solving a similar problem — handling variability in production deployments. My experience could help."

Make it relevant.

### Mistake 6: Reading from notes / sounding rehearsed
- Practice OUT LOUD, not just by writing
- Vary tone, pacing
- Show genuine emotion (excitement about the project, frustration with the bug, etc.)

## Practice protocol

### Per story (when developing):
1. Write in STAR format
2. Practice out loud 3-5 times
3. Get someone to give feedback (or record yourself + listen)
4. Refine: tighter, clearer, more specific
5. Time it: aim for 2-3 minutes max

### Per interview cycle (weekly during Phase 3-4):
1. Pick 5 random questions
2. Answer out loud (or record)
3. Identify weakest answer
4. Improve story for that question
5. Repeat

### With me (Claude):
- "Quiz me on Amazon's 16 LPs. Ask me one at random, push for follow-ups, score me at the end."
- "Mock-interview me. You're an Anthropic recruiter. 30 min behavioral round. Score me."
- "Critique this story I'm developing for 'tell me about a project that failed': [paste]"

## Recovery from a tough behavioral question

If you blank on a question:

1. **Pause + acknowledge:** "Good question, let me think for a moment."
2. **Buy time with clarifying question:** "Could you tell me a bit more about what aspect interests you?"
3. **Pivot if needed:** "I'll share a related story that touches on this..."

NEVER:
- Make something up
- Ramble while thinking
- Apologize excessively
